The Essay

We named it after the thing we were beginning to lose.

How Kizzuna started, what we're building with it, and exactly where the money goes.
By The Kizzuna Team

Kizzuna comes from the Japanese 絆, kizuna. It means the bond between people, the kind that only holds if both parties keep tending it. We picked the name before we had our finished film, a completed budget, or a forward vision, because it named the thing we thought was quietly going missing.

It started as a two-week digital detox in high school. A few students at Andover put their phones away, and the two weeks turned into a question we couldn't drop, which turned into a camera, which turned into a film shot across the country. Somewhere in there, it stopped being a project and became a greater purpose.

What we're building is not complicated. A documentary called Disconnected, made by people who grew up directly inside the problem rather than watching it from across a generation. And a program carrying this into schools, so the film begins the needed dialogue.

A film that screens once, gets a round of applause, and disappears has done nothing. •

We want the assembly, then the classroom session the week after, then the parent evening where somebody finally says the thing that was once quiet. This is the part that moves these students.

Kizzuna will begin going into classrooms this year, from presentations to mentorship programs. This is what the money is really for.

We recently won the Allstate Foundation College Service Grant, which backs youth-led service work across the country. This represents the largest single grant we have won thus far, and we look to build on this to fund our programs across the country.

We're an LLC, not a registered nonprofit, so giving us money is a gift and not a tax write-off. We say this in every issue because we believe you should know exactly where the potential money will be going. We're working on the fiscal sponsorship that would change it.

The goal this year is simple: Finish the film, and get it in front of our first ten to fifteen schools, on the way to a much bigger number we're not shy about. Four hundred.

Thanks for being here this early.

Ben, Kei & Valentin

Research

Why they'd rather tell a chatbot

Hopelab and Harvard's Center for Digital Thriving sat down with thirty people aged fourteen to twenty-two who use AI for emotional support. One reason came up more than any other: They didn't want to burden their friends. That isn't a technology problem. That's what happens when you decide your problems are too much for the people around you. Read →

The Numbers

One in eight became one in five

New research in JAMA Pediatrics found the share of young people going to AI chatbots for mental health advice jumped from roughly one in eight to one in five in a single year. The part worth sitting with is the researcher's unique framing: For a lot of teenagers, genuine care is difficult to find, expensive, or embarrassing to ask for. Read →

Policy

The bans are aimed at the wrong thing

Governments from Australia to the UK spent this year banning teenagers from social media. Meanwhile the chatbot is what the teens are actually getting stuck on, and the legislation has barely touched this. The UK's ban mentions AI companions once, and only the romantic ones. Read →

Try This

One screen, one evening.

Pick a night this week. Leave your phone in another room and let yourself have exactly one screen, or none at all. The TV counts. The first twenty minutes are rough, and we won't pretend they aren't. However, you will eventually realize something this night. Or maybe not. Hit reply and tell us what turned up for you. We read all of them at Kizzuna.
